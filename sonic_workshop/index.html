Welcome to the workshop

Music theory is a collection of ways to describe, organize, and explore sound. This prototype is the beginning of a living reference: chapters teach concepts, while instruments let you hear and manipulate them. It emphasizes common Western notation and harmony, without claiming they are the only musical systems.

Workshop design principle

One set of musical building blocks powers many experiences: pitch classes, interval patterns, rhythmic cycles, and audio scheduling. This is an expandable first volume, not a claim that every corner of music theory has already been documented.

BOOK I

Sound, Pitch & Notation

1. What is sound?

Sound begins with vibration. In air, pressure variations reach our ears. Repetition rate (frequency, measured in hertz) is closely related to perceived pitch; amplitude influences loudness, and the mixture of frequencies shapes timbre.

2. Octaves

Doubling a frequency produces an octave above: A4 at 440 Hz and A5 at 880 Hz are one octave apart. The ear often hears them as closely related pitches.

3. Twelve-tone equal temperament

In the common twelve-tone equal-tempered system, an octave is divided into twelve equal logarithmic steps, called semitones. Each semitone multiplies frequency by 2^(1/12). Other tuning systems exist and are important.

4. Written notes

A staff uses lines, spaces, clefs, noteheads, rests, accidentals, and time signatures to encode musical events. Letter names A–G repeat across octaves. Sharps and flats alter written pitches; enharmonic spellings can sound the same in equal temperament while serving different harmonic functions.

TRY IT: Open Scale Explorer, play C4 and C5, and listen for the octave relationship.

BOOK II

Rhythm, Meter & Polyrhythms

1. Pulse, tempo, and meter

A pulse is a regular reference beat. Tempo describes its rate. Meter groups beats into recurring patterns, often emphasizing certain positions. A time signature describes the notated grouping and beat-unit relationship; it is not itself a rhythm.

2. Subdivision

A beat may be divided into two, three, four, or other equal parts. A subdivision is a timing grid, not necessarily an audible hit.

3. Polyrhythm

A 5:4 polyrhythm fits five evenly spaced attacks and four evenly spaced attacks into the same time span. Both voices begin together and meet at the next cycle boundary. Their combined grid has 20 equal slots, although only some slots contain attacks.

4. Cross-rhythm versus changing meter

Simultaneous contrasting pulse divisions differ from simply switching between measures of different lengths. Musical context can make either feel ambiguous or fluid.

TRY IT: Set the reactor to 5:4, listen with both voices, then turn down the volume and try tapping the two parts independently.

BOOK III

Scales, Keys & Modes

1. Intervals are distances

An interval is a relationship between two pitches. In equal temperament, semitone distances offer a convenient way to model many familiar scales. Spelling matters too: C to D♯ and C to E♭ may sound identical on an equal-tempered keyboard but have different names and functions.

2. Major scale

The major scale follows the step pattern 2–2–1–2–2–2–1 semitones. From C, this produces C D E F G A B C.

3. Natural minor

The natural minor scale follows 2–1–2–2–1–2–2. From A, it produces A B C D E F G A.

4. Modes

Modes such as Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, and Locrian have distinct interval structures. The same set of pitch classes can take on a different musical center depending on context.

TRY IT: Compare C Ionian (major) and C Lydian. The raised fourth is the color change.

BOOK IV

Harmony & Chord Construction

1. Building a chord

A major triad consists of root, major third (4 semitones), and perfect fifth (7 semitones). A minor triad uses a minor third (3 semitones). Diminished and augmented triads alter the fifth.

2. Seventh chords

Adding another chord tone creates seventh chords. A dominant seventh is built from 0, 4, 7, and 10 semitones above the root; a major seventh uses 0, 4, 7, and 11.

3. Inversions

Moving a chord tone into another octave changes its voicing and bass note without necessarily changing the chord's pitch-class identity.

4. Function and context

In common-practice tonal harmony, tonic, predominant, and dominant functions describe characteristic harmonic tendencies. Other musical traditions organize harmony differently.

TRY IT: Play C major, C minor, and C diminished. Listen for how changing one interval alters the emotional color.

BOOK V

Composition & Musical Form

1. Motifs

A motif is a small recognizable musical idea. Composers develop motifs through repetition, variation, inversion, transposition, fragmentation, and rhythmic displacement.

2. Phrases and cadence

Musical phrases create expectations and points of rest or continuation. Cadences are one way tonal music articulates these boundaries.

3. Texture and form

Monophony, homophony, polyphony, and other textures describe relationships among musical lines. Larger forms such as binary, ternary, rondo, and verse–chorus organize sections over time.

4. Start composing

Try writing a four-note motif, repeating it with one changed rhythm, and answering it with a contrasting phrase. Composition can begin with rhythm just as easily as pitch.

TRY IT: Use the Metronome to keep time while improvising a motif on the Scale Explorer keyboard.

BOOK VI

Advanced Theory: First Doors

1. Modulation

Modulation changes the perceived tonal center. Common tones, pivot chords, chromatic motion, and direct key changes are some ways music establishes a new center.

2. Secondary dominants

A secondary dominant temporarily emphasizes a chord other than the home tonic. For example, D7 may point toward G in a piece centered on C.

3. Polymeter

Polymeter combines different metrical groupings. Unlike a simple n:m polyrhythm within a common span, parts may have distinct bar lengths that realign only after multiple measures.

4. Tuning and temperament

Equal temperament is a practical compromise, not a universal physical law. Just intonation, meantone, historical temperaments, and non-Western tuning traditions offer other interval relationships.
